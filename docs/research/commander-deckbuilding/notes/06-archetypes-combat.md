## 1. Core packages and counts by archetype

### Takeaway
No accessible source gives a complete package for any combat archetype in Commander. The sourced material is creature-count ranges, land counts and single-deck breakdowns: about 30 creatures for aggro and typal, 20 to 30 for Voltron, and 36 to 40 lands.

### Cited findings
- Overall Commander range: 20 to 45 creatures, with archetype the main driver; the article treats about 50 as the point to worry about [S1].
- Headline figures [S1]: aggro 30 or more; typal 30 or more; Voltron 20 to 30; control 20 to 30; combo 15 to 30; spellslinger 25 or fewer.
- Aggro wants many cheap, small creatures [S1]. A 60-card guide suggests 8 to 12 one-drops and 8 to 12 two-drops [S13].
- Go-wide has no separate creature figure. Token makers count as creature slots; token builds need doublers, sacrifice outlets or pump, and unsupported 1/1s can backfire [S1].
- Voltron, one deck [S11]: 18 creatures plus commander, about 20 auras (approximate), 27 enchantments in total, one artifact, no equipment, 34 lands, 8 instants and 11 sorceries. The extract's tally of these listed cards sums to 100.
- Aura advice [S11]: spread auras over several creatures so one removal spell does not end the plan; hold game-winning auras for late (the author's opinion).
- Commander-only plan [S9]: the commander is the sole win condition; cheap pump is central, and one-mana +4 power effects are rated highly.
- Card draw on damage [S9]: combat damage draws cards for the commander's controller, and the damaged opponent draws too; the author judges that manageable. The Uril deck has 11 card-draw spells, not itemized [S11].
- Typal, one deck [S12]: 26 creature cards plus commander, 11 anthems, four token-making creatures (plus Molten Echoes), three card-draw cards, and goad as the core loop. The article does not count members of the type.
- Lands: 36 to 40 for Commander [S8]; "around 36" as conventional wisdom and about 38 per the author [S2]; 37 to 40 lands and about 50 mana sources counting rocks [S10]; one SCG deck runs 40 [S4]; the Uril deck runs 34 [S11].
- Curve [S2]: overloading on 4+ mana cards is a warning; a few 5- and 6-drops are fine. One deck's spells average 1.56 mana value [S9].
- Extra combats: no source. The only sourced item is Fire Lord Azula's text: firebending 2 adds two red mana that lasts until end of combat, and her copy effect applies to spells cast while she attacks [S15].

### Inferences
- Voltron's 20 to 30 range and Uril's 18 creatures are compatible when auras carry the board.
- Go-wide has no sourced creature count, so its size has to follow its support pieces [S1].
- Most sources share the 36 to 40 land range; the 34 in S11 is the outlier.

### Gaps
- Go-wide anthem, doubler and token-maker counts: F1 failed; nothing else found.
- Aggro package counts: F2 failed; only S1 and the 60-card S13 remain.
- Voltron's five-item list: F7 returned no text.
- Extra-combat packages: no source.

## 2. Evasion in multiplayer

### Takeaway
The sources rank evasion one-on-one and never test pods. Some types beat small blockers, others big ones, and team-wide effects cover the whole attack.

### Cited findings
- Ranking [S3]: unblockable 1; flying 2; trample 4; protection 5; deathtouch 7; shadow 9; menace 10; intimidate 11; fear 12; daunt 13; flanking 14; "can't be blocked by" effects 16; stalking 17; nimble 18; burrowing 20; falter and bedlam 21; skulk 22. No scoring method is given.
- The article has no multiplayer section; Commander appears only as a product mention [S3].
- Rules text [S15]: fear, blocked only by artifact and/or black creatures; intimidate, blocked only by artifact creatures or creatures sharing a color; menace, needs two or more blockers; shadow, blocked only by shadow creatures; skulk, not blockable by greater power; nimble, not blockable by power 3 or more; flanking, each non-flanking blocker gets -1/-1.
- Menace gets past one big blocker and punishes opponents with few creatures [S3]. Falter makes a creature effectively unblockable against a single blocker [S3].
- Flanking kills 1/1 chump blockers [S3]; trample means excess damage gets through chump blocks [S3].
- Daunt (power 2 or less cannot block) is called incredibly useful against go-wide token decks [S3]. The phrase "power 2 or less" appears on 120 cards in the cache, some as blocker restrictions such as Doorman [S15]. The cache has no Daunt keyword card; keyword text unverified.
- Stalking caps blockers at one per attacker and guards against double or triple blocks by a 1/1 army [S3]. The cache has no Stalking keyword card; 34 cards contain "can't be blocked by more than one creature" [S15].
- Skulk is called not very popular and narrower than designers expected; nimble holds its value better as the creature is pumped [S3].
- Team-wide effects: Bedlam (creatures can't block) [S15] is pitched as giving every creature perfect evasion [S3]. Falter stops non-flyers from blocking that turn [S15]. Cover of Darkness gives fear to creatures of one chosen type [S15]. Siege Behemoth gives super-trample to the whole battlefield [S3]. Gimli's Reckless Might gives team haste and a fight when formidable [S15].
- Protection is one of the best ways to sneak creatures past blockers [S3].
- Owner [OG]: menace and "can't be blocked by big creatures" did nothing against eight 1/1 chump blockers. Ezio's combat-damage kill needs a player at 10 or less life and a five-color payment [S15].

### Inferences
- Menace needs two blockers, so its value falls as opponents add creatures. S3 says this directly, and [OG] matches it: eight chump blockers meet a two-blocker requirement easily.
- Skulk-type restrictions stop big blockers, not 1/1s; this matches [OG].
- Daunt-type restrictions target the chump-blocker class that S3 describes; their keyword text is unverified.
- Team-wide effects (Bedlam, Falter, team fear, team haste) scale with the size of the attack, because one card covers every attacker.
- Team fear is weak where many black and artifact creatures can block; how common they are in pods is unverified.

### Gaps
- No pod measurement of any evasion type.
- Frequency of black or artifact blockers in Commander pods: unverified.

## 3. Haste and summoning sickness

### Takeaway
Haste lets a creature attack the turn it enters. The only sourced reason is that opponents remove the commander before it can attack [S4]. The only sourced count is one SCG deck with six haste cards; no expert count was found.

### Cited findings
- Haste reminder text: the creature can attack and tap as soon as it comes under your control [S15].
- Haste sources in the cache [S15]: Fervor (creatures you control have haste); Rising of the Day (same, and +1/+0 for legendary creatures); Gimli's Reckless Might (same, and a fight when formidable at total power 8); Crashing Drawbridge (defender; tap to give your creatures haste); Lightning Greaves (equip 0; haste and shroud); Swiftfoot Boots (equip 1; haste and hexproof); Rhythm of the Wild (riot on nontoken creatures); Slayers' Stronghold (land; pump with haste and vigilance).
- One SCG Commander deck lists six haste cards, one copy each: Crashing Drawbridge, Swiftfoot Boots, Gimli's Reckless Might, Rhythm of the Wild, Rising of the Day and Slayers' Stronghold [S4].
- The article's reason: opponents are strongly motivated to remove the commander before it attacks, so haste lets it attack at once and repeatedly [S4].
- Owner [OG]: no creature had haste, and nothing gave haste.

### Inferences
- Equipment haste (Greaves, Boots) stays with the equipped creature and adds shroud or hexproof, combining haste with protection (inference from S15 and the removal reasoning in S4).
- Team haste on enchantments suits a board of freshly cast creatures; no source counts such cards.
- The six-card deck is one data point, not a target.

### Gaps
- No expert haste-count guideline for any archetype.

## 4. Protecting attackers and the commander; defense while tapped; recovery after wipes

### Takeaway
One MTGGoldfish article ranks protection first for a commander-plan deck and treats cheap hexproof and indestructible effects as central [S9]. Defense while tapped is sourced only as card text. Wipe-recovery principles are the best-sourced part of this question [S5].

### Cited findings
- Protection [S9]: the commander must survive because the whole plan depends on it; cheap hexproof and indestructible effects are central; single-keyword options are weaker but worth it for power.
- Protection pieces in one Voltron deck [S11]: Flickering Ward (protection from one color), Sejiri Shelter (instant defense), Sterling Grove (enchantments protected from destruction), Destiny Spinner (enchantments protected from counterspells), Faith's Reward (returns permanents after a sweeper), Grand Abolisher (stops interaction on the key turn), Three Dreams (finds protection or evasion). Some are labeled opinion in the article.
- Removal pooling [S6]: players can team up to strip a threat's protection, Lightning Greaves being the example, and then answer it.
- Removal magnets and retaliation [S7]: cards that make you a target (The One Ring, Sheoldred) and retaliatory targeting, which mostly rewards control decks and sours the table. Let others make the first big play.
- Defense while tapped: S9 says extra vigilance or lifelink helps because its deck's blocking is thin [S9]. Vigilance: attacking does not tap the creature [S15]. Baird: creatures can't attack you unless their controller pays 1 per attacking creature [S15]. No expert count.
- Wipe baseline [S5]: about 3 to 5 for an average Commander deck; creature decks should run fewer because wipes hit their own creatures.
- Wipes and attackers: wipe plus indestructible clears the way for the commander [S9]. A -X/-X sweeper such as Languish lets big creatures survive [S5]. Synergy wipes, the article's top-ranked type, hit opponents' key permanents while yours survive [S5].
- Recovery [S5]: sorcery-speed wipes give opponents the first chance to rebuild; instant-speed wipes in an opponent's end step let you rebuild at once; mass bounce buys about two turns; exiled cards are harder to recover; too many wipes lead to constant rebuilding.
- Token decks and wipes [S5]: wraths hurt tokens even when the token player casts them; mass edicts hurt token decks too; synergy wipes suit them best.
- Owner [OG]: his creatures were fragile, and he was wide open after attacking.

### Inferences
- A sourced protection stack covers two threats: one answer to targeted removal (hexproof or shroud) and one to damage or destruction (indestructible or protection) (inference from S9 and S15).
- Wipe-plus-indestructible [S9] and synergy wipes [S5] are the two sourced ways to clear a board without losing your own attackers.
- Vigilance and attack taxes are the only sourced defensive tools; how many a Commander list needs is unknown.

### Gaps
- Protection counts for combat decks: F3 failed; no other doctrine found.
- Defense-while-tapped counts: none.
- Recovery cards for creature decks: S5 gives principles, not a card list.

## 6. Typal decks

### Takeaway
One typal deck is sourced in detail [S12]; the archetype figure is 30 or more creatures [S1]. The sources answer wide boards with sweepers that spare the deck's own creatures, anthems and a cap on theme. No source measures how often typal decks lose to wraths.

### Cited findings
- Typal [S1]: 30 or more creatures; lords and payoffs need a dense board; big-creature tribes run a few strong threats instead.
- Marisi [S12]: 11 anthems across creature, artifact and enchantment types; the subtype count is not given. The core loop needs two or three attackers and goads each combat. Board wipes are used sparingly; a sweeper was added after testing showed lopsided wipes singled out the deck's own board. Big blockers are not addressed; one card limits attackers to one blocker each.
- Theme cap [S10, 2017]: "too much theme, too few answers" is a named mistake; the fix is about 75 percent of nonland cards on theme, with a floor near half, plus reserved slots for interaction. The example split is 37 on-theme cards, 13 catch-all slots and about 50 mana sources.
- Self-hosers and interaction [S2]: wraths that hit your own creatures, and cutting removal for an all-out attack, are listed mistakes.
- Wide boards [S7]: a wide board one or two attacks from lethal counts among the greatest threats; a sweeper such as Toxic Deluge answers an early rush.
- F4 (the Card Kingdom typal article) failed.

### Inferences
- A typal list against wide boards needs three things the sources name separately: a sweeper that spares its own creatures [S5], cheap removal [S2] and evasion that ignores chump blockers [S3]. No source combines the three; the combination is an inference.
- The 75 percent theme cap [S10] is a 2017 opinion; treat it as historical.

### Gaps
- No sourced member-count guideline for typal decks.
- No data on typal losses to wraths.

## 7. Failure modes in pods and expert fixes

### Takeaway
Sourced failure modes: sweepers that hit the deck's own creatures, too little interaction, attack targets chosen without a threat read, thin mana, and win conditions that cannot finish games. Sourced fixes: interaction that spares the deck, a threat read before each attack, one plan with multi-role cards, and 36 to 40 lands with ramp.

### Cited findings
- Mistake list [S2]: self-hosing wraths; cutting removal for an all-out attack; picking attack targets by die roll; poor threat assessment; coming unprepared for tokens, counters and life totals; colorless utility lands in four- or five-color decks; too many 4+ drops; too few lands.
- Other mistakes [S10]: too little mana (fix: 37 to 40 lands plus ramp); too much theme; too little card advantage.
- Table-level lessons [S14]: a sweeper-heavy ramp deck made pods concede; a drain-only win condition could not finish games and drew attacks on its commander; too many subsystems failed; test cards from every seat.
- Attack targets [S7]: attack the player aiming for the longest game; do not pile onto a single answered threat; a deal with the leader tends to feed the leader; a go-wide deck can finish about three opponents near 20 life, but several at full life are much tougher.
- Table politics [S6]: with three or more players you often choose between two targets; a runaway leader draws a gang-up; removal holders can pool their answers.
- Wipes in pods [S5]: a Damnation-style wipe is a three-for-one in a duel and a nine-for-one in a four-player pod where each opponent has three creatures.
- Protection priority [S9]: losing the commander repeatedly is unaffordable, and the deck's blocking is thin.
- Owner [OG]: wide open after attacking; fragile creatures; no haste; menace did nothing; team-wide evasion and a conditional tribal draw spell were missing.

### Inferences
- Owner losses map to sourced failure modes: "wide open after attacking" matches S9's admitted thin blocking and S2's attack-first mindset; "fragile creatures" matches S9's protection priority; "menace did nothing" matches S3's count-dependence; "no haste" matches S4's reason.
- Order suggested by the sources (inference): (1) protect the win condition [S9]; (2) sweepers and removal that spare the deck [S5, S2]; (3) evasion that fits the table's blocker profile [S3]; (4) 36 to 40 lands with ramp [S8, S10]; (5) one plan with multi-role cards [S14].

### Gaps
- Defense after attacking: no accessible source; F1 to F3 failed.
- Pod win rates for any archetype: none.

## Conflicts between sources
- Land count: S8 (36 to 40) and S2 (about 36 to 38) against S11 (34). S10 (37 to 40) also counts about 50 mana sources including rocks, a different measure.
- Voltron creatures: S1 gives 20 to 30; the S11 list has 18.

- Evasion ranking: S3 ranks menace tenth yet says it punishes boards with few creatures; the ranking ignores board size.
- Internal: S2's title says 15 mistakes; the extract shows 16 headings.
- Age: S10 and S13 date from 2017; S8 and S11 from 2022 (updated 2025); the rest from 2024 to 2026.
