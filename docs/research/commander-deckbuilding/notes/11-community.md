## 1. Process and core ratios

**Takeaway.** The sources agree on the order: fix the commander and the plan; name functional slots (lands, ramp, draw, removal, plan); fill them with staples, then deck-specific cards; cut last. The recurring numbers are 36 to 40 lands and about 10 each of ramp, draw and removal. The one measured Bracket 3 median (S15) is 36 lands and 13 ramp pieces. Every ratio is a starting point, not a rule.

- Workflow [S3, Opinion]. The series starts from the deck idea, then a deck page with custom categories (strategy, land, draw, ramp, removal) and placeholder slots, ten each for ramp, draw and removal in its example. Staples fill the slots, deck-specific cards replace them, and cutting comes later (part 5, not read).
- Chain from what the card needs [S3, Opinion]. Ask what each card wants and how to get it, then chain from the commander's needs (for example, creatures in the graveyard need self-mill or tutors) to find supporting cards.
- Final check [S3, Convention in the community]. Re-read the commander's EDHREC page as a last check for omissions. The author attributes the name "idiot check" to another podcast.
- Lands [S4, 2013 anecdote]. The opening post's 36 to 40 lands and 5 to 10 ramp is described as something the poster heard. One commenter says to start at 40 lands and drop one land per two ramp sources; no source is given, so this is an unchecked rule of thumb. Another commenter: more lands if the ramp is land-based, fewer if it is artifact-based [Opinion].
- Measured Bracket 3 medians [S15, Data; 1,285 decks with self-declared brackets; complete 98 to 102-card lists; counts from Scryfall tags and text matching]: 36 lands (middle half 34 to 37), ramp 13 (10 to 18), draw 10 (7 to 13), spot removal 5 (3 to 7), board wipes 2 (1 to 4), counterspells 1 (0 to 3), tutors 4 (2 to 7), Game Changers 2 (0 to 3), average mana value 3.11 (2.83 to 3.44).
- Brackets 1 to 4 share one skeleton in the same survey: medians of 35 to 37 lands and 10 to 14 ramp; the author's reading is that power changes more than structure [S15, Data].
- Definitions matter. S15 counts rocks, fast mana and rituals as ramp, and counts as draw any draw engine or card that draws two or more cards. S4's 5 to 10 ramp comes from a forum thread and defines nothing.
- Simulation [S14, Data; 50,000 games per configuration; London mulligan with the free first mulligan; ramp output modelled; no tutors, card draw, fetch lands or interaction]. A midrange deck (average mana value 3.0, 10 ramp, three colours) reaches 95% or more on-curve casting through five mana at 36 to 37 lands. A midrange deck with four rocks at 29 lands casts its 5-drop on turn 5 in 88.4% of games, against 95.6% at 37 lands. Going from 37 to 40 lands adds about 1 to 3 points per spell.
- Secondary figures [S14, Secondary, unverified]. ScrollVault says EDHREC's average decks for the top 100 commanders per bracket have a median of 35 lands (500 decks, collected 2026-09-07), and that 53 of the 70 precons it counted run 37 or 38 lands. It quotes a Karsten regression for 99-card decks: lands = 31.42 + 3.13 × average mana value − 0.28 × (ramp and draw). Karsten's own article was not reached (X6), so this formula is unverified.
- Inference (not sourced). S14's midrange threshold and S15's Bracket 3 median agree on 36 to 37 lands. The 40-minus-rocks rule from S4 is older and untested.

## 2. Ratios by archetype

**Takeaway.** The only archetype numbers I read come from a simulation (S14) and a survey (S15). They say the land count should follow the curve and the engine: landfall and high-curve decks run 38 to 40 lands, while fast cEDH shells run 28 to 31 with heavy acceleration. Sources for the owner's other archetype lessons (haste for attackers, draw density for instant and sorcery decks, defence while tapped) were not found.

- Lands by archetype [S14, Data; "recommended" means its cast-rate threshold, not a win rate]:
  - cEDH turbo: 29 to 31 lands with 12 ramp sources including fast mana. The page says its simulated list includes Mana Crypt, which it notes is banned in Commander.
  - Combo: 33 to 35 lands with about 10 rocks; the goal is finding the pieces, not flooding.
  - Midrange (average mana value 3.0, 10 mixed ramp): 36 to 37.
  - Battlecruiser (average mana value 3.5 or more, 8 ramp): 38 to 40. A 7-drop is castable by turn 7 in 72.8% of games at 37 lands and 84.0% at 42.
  - Landfall (6 land-ramp spells such as Cultivate and Kodama's Reach in place of rocks): 38 to 40. Five-drops on turn 5 reach 94.0% at 36 lands and 96.8% at 40.
- Conflict. X8 (snippet, unverified) gives 40 to 42 lands for landfall; S14 gives 38 to 40.
- Voltron [S16, Opinion]. Run 8 to 12 mana rocks to hit land drops and cast the commander on turn three. Choose auras or equipment; the guide calls equipment the safer multiplayer choice because its pieces stay when the creature dies.
- Landfall, in the owner's terms. S14 supports more lands and land-based ramp, which also triggers landfall. That is a shape of "more ramp" but not a claim about total ramp counts. S14 is a simulation of casting, not of winning.
- Not found in what I read: haste for attackers; draw density for instant and sorcery decks; team evasion beyond Voltron's evasion piece; defence while tapped (blockers, vigilance). These stay open.

## 3. Consistency: plans, redundancy, tutors, mulligans

**Takeaway.** The consistency sources agree on one declared plan, enough pieces of that plan, redundancy in the pieces it needs, enablers for finding them, and repeated play with the same deck. Only one numeric rule appears (an engine card needs about ten synergy cards or many tutors), and it is a commenter's anecdote.

- Layering [S3, Opinion]. A card one step from the commander or plan is usually fine. Cards two or more steps away become dead draws because their enablers are missing. The author's example, a chain of reasonable additions (taxes, counterspells, planeswalkers, proliferate, tokens, graveyard recursion, ramp), shows how a deck drifts. A reader's reply concedes that a deck with many draw spells can afford one card further out, at a cost to focus [Opinion].
- Keyed synergy [S8, Opinion]. Key low-profile synergies off the commander: they work more often than two random cards that might meet by chance. The author's build (c. 2009) sorted candidates into piles by function (high toughness, toughness boosters, walls, creatures with reach, others), cut to the plan, then added utility draw.
- Colour balance [S8, Convention in the source]. Count the coloured symbols in every casting cost. The example moved from 13 black, 27 green and 42 white to 26, 28 and 27 by cutting the heaviest white cards.
- Stand-alone pieces [S10, cEDH; labelled]. The article describes the best compact cEDH packages as having pieces that are useful on their own; its example is a commander with a mana rock, described as useful independently. It also says most blue-black Thassa's Oracle lists run both Demonic Consultation and Tainted Pact for redundancy [Convention in cEDH].
- Engine cards [S4, anecdote]. Leave an engine card out unless you run many tutors or about ten cards that synergise with it. The commenter's ten-percent chance estimate is his own and unchecked.
- Tutors [S3, S9, S10, Opinion]. S3 says the plan's consistency comes from tutors and card draw; S9 says consistency is the fastest route to victory. S10 rates combo lines on five axes (speed, card efficiency, tutorability, resilience, deckbuilding cost). That is its own scale, not a standard.
- Protection and sequencing for Voltron [S16, Opinion]. Cast the commander only when protection is ready; reserve tutor slots for protection; the guide treats power, evasion and protection as the three pillars.
- Mulligans [S13, Rule and Convention]. In multiplayer the first mulligan is free (rule 103.5e, cited by the guide). Conventional wisdom is not to go below five cards. Judge a hand by curve, colour, plan (does it advance the deck's plan?), sequence, and interaction (does the table need proactive or reactive cards?). A hand of three nonland permanents with too few lands is a "house of cards" to mulligan [Opinion]. The guide lists house rules and the arguments for and against unlimited mulligans without a verdict.
- Experience [S9, Opinion]. Playing one deck often improves mulligan choices and the timing of the win. Goldfishing alone does not show how the deck fares in an unknown pod. The stated costs are boredom, losing streaks and the loss of surprise value from other decks.
- Inference (not sourced). The owner's gate (one declared plan, enough pieces of each function, redundancy) is supported in direction by S3, S8 and S16. No source gives piece counts per function.

## 4. Multiplayer realities

**Takeaway.** The multiplayer sources agree on threat assessment: judge threats by engines, boards and protection rather than life totals; manage the social side; do not spend every answer on one board; and close by doing the damage arithmetic while watching the table's open mana.

- Life total is the clock, not the score [S6, Opinion; widely repeated]. The real threats are growing creatures, resource engines (cards and mana, including tokens that hide mana), and layers of protection. Protection layers need artifact and enchantment removal, which the author says is under-represented in deck lists [Opinion].
- Three layers of threat assessment [S5, Opinion, citing Rosewater's categories]: comprehension, board and strategic complexity, plus social deduction. Players avoid early attacks and spread damage so they do not look like the threat [S5, S6, Opinion].
- Salt [S5, Opinion]. Much frustration comes from poor threat assessment. Removing the same commander again and again is taboo in many groups. The author calls running enough removal the accurate reading, but calls removing one commander four times unfun.
- Answers [S5, Opinion]. A player who spends every answer on one board leaves the next player with a win in hand unstopped. Track the next threat in turn order. S17 makes the same point as a closing tactic.
- Politics [S7, Opinion]. Never break a deal, because reputation carries across games. Make deal wording precise (attack versus target). Tempting-offer cards are prisoner's dilemmas where the first choosers set the tone. Banding together against an archenemy is a normal deal. The author refuses to play again with anyone who concedes a game to deny life gain (the strategic scoop) and says groups differ on it.
- Griefers and battle lines [S8, Opinion]. Cards that annoy the whole table without ending the game (global locks, shuffle hazards, all-lands destruction) draw battle lines, and the group then gangs up on the player. Do not treat opponents as opponents until necessary, and do not start fights for no reason.
- Voltron in a pod [S16, Opinion]. The big creature makes its controller the archenemy. Hold back until the commander is protected. Attack one chosen opponent at a time. Finishers that hit several players, and extra combat steps, matter because one kill per turn loses to three opponents.
- Closing [S17, Opinion]. Name the two or three real ways your deck ends games (combat, combo, drain) before the game starts. Add up the damage out loud. Do not spend a turn on card draw when you have lethal, because three opponents draw roughly three answers for each card you see. Count open mana before the kill. In a four-player pod a 60% attempt can beat waiting.
- Statistics [X10, Snippet, unverified]. A search summary attributes to playgroup.gg a median multiplayer game of about 58 minutes and about half of multiplayer wins from combat. Not opened.
- Wipes [S15, Data; S17, Opinion]. The median Bracket 3 deck runs two board wipes (middle half 1 to 4). A lethal attack invites a wipe, so plan protection before the swing.
- Not found: wide boards of chump blockers; big creatures as a defence problem; defence while tapped; closing against three opponents with blockers. See Gaps.

## 5. Common mistakes, and diagnosing a deck that does nothing

**Takeaway.** The mistakes that recur are: too many layers or plans; too much visible power; engine cards without enablers; value spells that delay the kill; griefer cards; too few lands for the curve; and no named finisher. The "deck does nothing" diagnosis is not sourced: the search returned mostly spam pages.

- Too many layers [S3, Opinion]. See section 3.
- Too much power in front of the table [S8, Opinion]. High-power cards draw focus; a united front beats any single strong card.
- Engine cards without enablers [S4, anecdote].
- Value trap [S17, Opinion]. A draw spell that does not advance the kill gives the table time to find answers.
- Griefer cards [S8, Opinion].
- Too few lands for a high curve [S14, Data]. At 29 lands a four-rock midrange deck loses about seven points on the turn-5 five-drop against 37 lands.
- Over-ramp without payoffs [X8, Snippet, unverified].
- No named finisher [S17, Opinion; S16 for Voltron].
- Playing a deck for the first time in a pod [S9, Opinion]. The author treats the first game with a new deck as a test, not a plan.
- Diagnosis, from what was read: log the games and see where losses cluster [S17, Opinion]; check whether the opening hands carry the plan [S13]; count the enablers for each engine [S4, anecdote]; check the land count against the curve [S14, Data].
- "Pet cards" as a mistake: no source found. S3 uses the phrase for niche cards that fit a deck well.

## 6. Testing and tuning

**Takeaway.** Goldfishing is the default, and the sources agree it misses interaction and the pod. Their answers are: simulate interaction, log real games, and build experience with the same deck. The "cut the worst card" step is announced in S3's part 5, which was not read.

- Survey [S11, Data, self-selected]. In the author's poll of almost 1,000 Commander players, over 80% goldfish their decks in some way, and about 18% play two or more decks against each other.
- Thunderbowl [S11, the author's proposal]. A d20 table stands in for opponent actions (wipes, damage, artifact or enchantment removal; counterspells optional), so solo games carry stakes and interaction. Caveats: decks that depend on opponents for their plan (forced combat, politics, clones) need a different test.
- Goldfishing limits [S9, Opinion]. You cannot know the pod or the turn order in advance, and experience predicts mulligans better than solo runs.
- Logs [S17, Opinion]. Record a month of pod games and check whether losses cluster in the long games. The author suggests the pilot's patience is often the problem, not the list.
- Ramp by testing [S4, Opinion]. Playtest extensively to find the right ramp count; that was the thread's common answer.
- Tools and vendor material. S1 links to hypergeometric and mana-base calculators and to a playtesting episode (not read). S12 is a vendor's playtest blog with little method.
- Show-note cues [S2, S1; chapter titles only]. Testing, finding the pain point, playing the cards you actually play. The titles show what the hosts cover; the text does not say what they conclude.

## Conflicts between sources

- Land counts. S4 (2013 anecdote) gives 36 to 40 with a 40-minus-rocks rule. S14 and S15 (2026) give 36 to 37 for midrange and a Bracket 3 median of 36. S8 (c. 2009) built 34 lands with several mana creatures and rocks (an example, not a rule). X8 (snippet) says 40 to 42 for landfall; S14 says 38 to 40.
- Ramp totals. S4's 5 to 10 (2013) against S15's Bracket 3 median of 13 (10 to 18). Part of the gap is definition: S15 counts rocks, fast mana and rituals. X7 (snippet, unverified) says 8 to 12.
- Mana rocks. S4 (one commenter) dislikes rocks for their complexity and for the threats they replace. S16 says run 8 to 12 rocks for Voltron. S14 treats rocks as ramp in cEDH. This is opinion against opinion; the data do not measure complexity.
- The commander. S9 says the commander matters less than playstyle and efficient win conditions. S8 says the commander is the most reliable card in a singleton deck and must be chosen with care. Different emphases; both are opinions.
- Politics against threat. S7 and S8 advise restraint and clear deals. S5 says the accurate threat is whatever is in your way, and calls repeated removal of one commander unfun. These fit if you target the threat, not the person; the weight differs.
- Focus of answers. S5 warns against spending all answers on one board; S16 tells a Voltron deck to pick one opponent. Removal and damage are different resources, so this is not a true contradiction.
- Tutors. S3, S9 and S10 treat tutors as consistency. X11 (snippet, unverified) is a 2020 forum opinion that tutors push builds toward combo lines at the cost of synergy.
- Unlimited mulligans. S13 lists both sides and gives no verdict.
- Playtesting. S11 (most players goldfish) and S9 (goldfishing is not enough) agree in direction and differ on what a good test looks like.
- Age. S4 (2013) and S8 (c. 2009) predate most of today's cards and some rules. Treat them as historical opinion.
- cEDH against Bracket 3. S15's cEDH median (28 lands, 21 ramp, 9 tutors, 13 Game Changers) describes a different shape. S9 and S10 are cEDH sources. Do not transfer cEDH figures to Bracket 3 without a label.
